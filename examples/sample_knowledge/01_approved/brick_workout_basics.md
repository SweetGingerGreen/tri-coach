# 核心摘要 (Summary)
A brick workout pairs a bike session immediately with a run, training the neuromuscular and pacing skills required to run well off the bike in a triathlon.

# 正文内容 (Content)
The most distinctive moment in a triathlon is the first few minutes of the run, when the legs have just spent one to several hours pushing pedals and have to suddenly absorb landing impact again. Brick workouts are designed to rehearse exactly that transition. The benefit is part neuromuscular (the legs learn the gait pattern under fatigue), part pacing (the athlete calibrates what race effort actually feels like off the bike), and part logistical (transitions and fueling get practiced).

A useful spectrum of brick designs covers different training goals. A short brick — for example, 60 to 90 minutes of bike followed by a 10 to 20 minute easy run — focuses on the neuromuscular adaptation and can be done weekly without major fatigue cost. A race-pace brick rehearses target effort on both legs of the workout and is typically reserved for the specific preparation block. A long brick combines a substantial bike session with a moderate run and is the most demanding form; it should be infrequent and followed by a real recovery day.

Pitfalls are well known. Stacking long bricks back to back accumulates fatigue faster than most amateurs realize and is a common cause of overuse injury, especially in the calves, Achilles, and hip flexors. The run portion of a brick is not the place to chase a workout that the standalone run schedule did not call for; if the legs are heavy, the right response is usually to shorten or skip the run, not to grind through it. Brick frequency typically increases in the final eight to twelve weeks before a long-course race, but never at the expense of recovery days or a manageable weekly high-intensity budget.
